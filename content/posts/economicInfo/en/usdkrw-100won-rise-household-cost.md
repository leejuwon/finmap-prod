- USD/KRW expresses Korean won per US dollar, so a higher quote means a weaker won against the dollar.
- A hypothetical move from 1,300 to 1,400 raises the won cost of an unchanged dollar bill by about 7.69% before fees.
- The percentage effect of a 100-won move depends on the starting exchange rate.
- Overseas travel and dollar-priced online purchases can reflect conversion changes at payment or settlement.
- The rate on a market screen is not necessarily the rate used for your card transaction or currency exchange.
- A Korean retailer's price does not automatically increase by the same percentage as USD/KRW.
- Import contracts, hedges, inventory, domestic costs, and competition can delay or limit pass-through.
- Dollar commodity prices and USD/KRW can reinforce or offset each other.
- Identify the unpaid dollar exposure before estimating a household or business cost change.

## A higher USD/KRW quote buys fewer dollars with the same won

At 1,300 KRW per USD, one dollar costs 1,300 won before fees; at 1,400 it costs 1,400. The won has weakened. A higher quote increases dollar purchasing costs for someone funding the bill in won.

The increase is 100 ÷ 1,300, about 7.69%. From 1,000 to 1,100 it would be 10%; from 1,500 to 1,600, about 6.67%. Returning from 1,400 to 1,300 is a 7.14% decline because the starting base differs.

The reciprocal USD-per-KRW percentage uses a different base. For a fixed dollar bill funded in won, use KRW per USD. The [exchange-rate fundamentals guide](/en/posts/economicInfo/fx-basics) covers the broader mechanics.

| Hypothetical dollar bill | At 1,300 KRW/USD | At 1,400 KRW/USD | Additional won before fees |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| $100 online purchase | ₩130,000 | ₩140,000 | ₩10,000 |
| $2,000 travel budget | ₩2,600,000 | ₩2,800,000 | ₩200,000 |
| $10,000 import invoice | ₩13,000,000 | ₩14,000,000 | ₩1,000,000 |

Each row rises 7.69% because the dollar bill is unchanged and fully exposed. A whole trip, retail product, or CPI basket contains other components and need not rise by that percentage.

## Three variables describe three stages of the price chain

The first variable is USD/KRW. Record its convention, time, and type: a market reference, customer bank rate, and card-settlement rate need not match.

The second variable is Korea's won-denominated import price index. It measures the import stage. Compare the corresponding contract-currency series where available, but do not treat the difference as a complete causal estimate for every import.

The third variable is the relevant domestic consumer price or CPI category. Later production, distribution, and domestic costs intervene; national CPI does not consist entirely of dollar-priced imports.

The [BOK September 2026 import/export price and trade-index release](https://www.bok.or.kr/portal/bbs/B0000501/view.do?nttId=11065605&menuNo=201264) is dated October 16 and labeled provisional. No attached numerical index values are quoted here. In [ECOS](https://ecos.bok.or.kr/), check month, currency basis, and revision status.

An index level is neither an exchange quote nor a percentage change. Use a consistent base-year series and reference month; a release-day currency move need not appear in that month's index.

## Direct dollar payments and domestic retail prices run on different clocks

| Cost exposure | When the effect may appear | What can alter it |
|---|---|---|
| Unpaid dollar hotel or travel bill | When the relevant conversion occurs | Prepayment, currency held, payment terms, bank spread |
| Dollar-priced online purchase | According to card or payment-provider processing terms | Processing date, network and issuer charges, refunds, shipping and taxes |
| Importer's new dollar invoice | Under the invoice and settlement contract | Hedging, supplier pricing, credit terms, shipping and insurance |
| Goods sold from existing Korean inventory | As stock is replaced or prices are reviewed | Earlier purchase costs, inventory policy, promotions, margins |
| Domestic services using imported inputs | Through later cost and pricing decisions | Imported-input share, wages, rent, competition, regulation |

These are possible timings, not a fixed schedule. One seller may reprice using replacement costs before old stock sells; another may absorb the increase. A bill already fixed in won can leave currency risk with the seller.

Authorization, processing, and settlement can occur on different dates. The applicable rule depends on issuer, network, currency, and product. Read the actual terms rather than assuming the authorization quote is final.

For a hypothetical single 1.2% proportional fee, a $100 bill at 1,400 becomes 100 × 1,400 × 1.012 = ₩141,680; at 1,410 it becomes ₩142,692. This invented fee is not a provider quote. Real fees can have several components and bases.

The [Visa exchange-rate calculator](https://www.visa.co.uk/support/consumer/travel-support/exchange-rate-calculator.html) gives an indication, not a guaranteed final charge. If offered home-currency billing, compare the actual rate and fees. Shipping, applicable taxes, and return costs also need transaction-specific checks.

> Checkpoint: calculate the unpaid dollar exposure, add actual payment terms, and assess retail pass-through separately.

## Inventory, contracts, and hedges interrupt a simple pass-through story

The chain is conversion → import cost → production and distribution → pricing decision → household payment. Each step introduces contracts or decisions. Pass-through need not be immediate, complete, or symmetric when currency direction reverses.

A hedge protects a defined amount and maturity, not all future costs. Earlier inventory and fixed-price contracts can delay or redistribute exposure; replacement purchases may face a different rate.

Domestic wages, rent, distribution, and margins are also part of the retail price. Applying the currency change to the entire price ignores those components and any change in the foreign supplier's price.

Competition and demand influence cost recovery. A seller may absorb costs, change suppliers or package sizes, or reprice selectively. An exporter receiving dollars can still face higher dollar input costs, so depreciation affects businesses differently.

## Oil and the exchange rate can reinforce or offset each other

A hypothetical $80 barrel at 1,300 converts to ₩104,000. At 1,400 it becomes ₩112,000, up 7.69%. With oil also rising to $88, it becomes ₩123,200, up 18.46%. The currency and commodity effects compound.

At $72 and 1,400, the conversion is ₩100,800, down about 3.08% from ₩104,000 despite a weaker won. These input examples exclude freight, insurance, refining, taxes, and distribution; they are not pump-price forecasts.

The [Korea oil-to-FX transmission guide](/en/posts/economicInfo/oil-shock-to-usdkrw-korea-transmission) covers wider channels. Keep currency and dollar commodity changes separate before assessing the retail bill.

| Scenario, not a forecast | What to observe | Potential household effect | Evidence that would change the interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Won weakens, dollar commodities broadly stable | USD/KRW, matching import-price currency bases, new invoice terms | Higher exposed dollar payments; later and uneven domestic effects | Supplier discounts, hedges, or sustained margin absorption offset the conversion pressure |
| Won weakens and dollar commodities rise | Both rate and input-price changes, freight and replacement costs | Reinforcing input-cost pressure and potentially broader pass-through | Commodity reversal, lower demand, contract protection, or reduced imported-input exposure |
| Won stabilizes or strengthens, retail response is delayed | New import costs versus older inventory and contract renewal dates | Dollar payments may ease sooner than shelf prices | Comparable retail prices actually fall, or renewed cost pressure removes expected relief |

These are testable conditions, not promised schedules. Match reference periods across several observations; a one-day reversal does not establish that monthly import or retail prices have fallen.

## Two people can face the same quote and different exposure

In the first hypothetical case, a traveler prepaid $1,200 of a $2,000 budget. Only $800 remains exposed: a 100-won rise adds ₩80,000 before fees, not ₩200,000. Check that prepayment is actually settled; won-fixed trip costs are separate.

In the second case, an importer's unpaid $10,000 invoice has a gross ₩1 million conversion difference. Hedges, dollar revenue, and pricing power determine the net effect. Unlike a traveler buying a finished service, the importer faces a further cost and pricing chain.

For residents of Korea, earning won and owing dollars is different from earning dollars and paying won rent. Existing balances and future liabilities matter. USD/KRW remains the relevant currency pair in both language versions.

## Five claims that overstate what a weaker won tells you

### Misconception 1: A 100-won increase always means the same percentage shock

Divide by the starting rate. A 100-won move from 1,000 is 10%, while the same move from 1,500 is about 6.67%. State both endpoints when comparing news events.

### Misconception 2: Every domestic price rises as much as USD/KRW

Only the exposed foreign-currency component experiences the direct conversion change. Input prices, domestic cost shares, hedges, and margins affect the next stages. National CPI cannot be calculated by copying a currency percentage.

### Misconception 3: A card charge uses the rate visible when I tap

The applicable timing and fees depend on the actual product and transaction terms. Authorization and final processing need not produce the same rate. Use the statement to reconcile the charge.

### Misconception 4: A stronger won immediately reverses every shelf-price increase

Inventory and contracts can carry earlier costs forward. Other costs may remain elevated, and pricing responses may differ between increases and decreases. Check actual comparable retail prices instead of assuming symmetry.

### Misconception 5: Won depreciation helps every exporter and hurts every other business

Revenue currency, imported inputs, foreign-currency debt, hedges, and demand differ. A company can receive more won per dollar of revenue while paying more for dollar inputs. Sector labels alone do not measure its net exposure.

## Build a cost estimate before making a currency decision

In fifteen minutes, list unpaid dollar bills, due dates, and existing dollar balances. Calculate a range with two labeled hypothetical rates, keeping prepaid and won-fixed items separate.

With an hour, check spreads, charges, and processing dates in the actual payment terms. Compare full delivered costs with local alternatives, including applicable taxes and return terms.

Over a week, collect comparable quotes with their rates and terms. Use matching months and currency bases in BOK data, while keeping your payment deadline separate from the release schedule.

## Questions about USD/KRW and household costs

### What does a rise in USD/KRW mean?

It means more Korean won are needed to buy one US dollar. Under this quote convention the won has weakened against the dollar. The effect on a person depends on the currencies of income and unpaid expenses.

### How much is a move from 1,300 to 1,400?

It raises the won cost of an unchanged dollar bill by about 7.69% before fees. Divide 100 by the starting rate of 1,300. These rates are hypothetical examples, not current market quotes.

### Does a $100 purchase cost exactly 140,000 won at 1,400?

That is the simple conversion before spreads, fees, shipping, and applicable taxes. The final charge depends on the actual payment terms and rate used for processing. The 1.2% fee illustration in this article is not a quoted provider fee.

### Which date determines a card's conversion rate?

It depends on the issuer, network, product, currency, and processing terms. Do not assume the authorization date always determines the final charge. Check the specific terms and reconcile them with the statement.

### Why can Korean retail prices move later than the exchange rate?

Inventory, supply contracts, hedging, domestic costs, and pricing decisions intervene between conversion and the retail sale. Pass-through can be delayed or incomplete. Some businesses may price using replacement costs before old inventory is exhausted.

### Can oil become cheaper in won even if the won weakens?

Yes. A sufficiently large decline in the dollar oil price can outweigh a higher KRW-per-dollar quote. Multiply the two components before considering freight, refining, taxes, and retail distribution.

### Does a 100-won move affect my whole travel budget?

Only costs that remain exposed to dollar conversion have the direct effect. Prepaid expenses, existing currency balances, and bills fixed in won should be separated. An unpaid $800 balance has an 80,000-won difference for a 100-won move before fees.

### Does a lower import price index guarantee cheaper groceries immediately?

No. Import and retail prices describe different stages. Check currency basis, month, contracts, and actual shelf prices before assuming immediate pass-through.

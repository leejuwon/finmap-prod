## Define “mortgage risk” the practical way: the break point, not the rate
Most people define mortgage risk as “rates went up.” That’s too vague to be useful. A workable definition is:

<strong>Your mortgage is risky if one realistic shock forces a non-negotiable sacrifice: essentials, family stability, or the ability to keep your plan running.</strong>

To make that measurable, you need three thresholds:

1) <strong>Target cap</strong>: payment you can carry while still saving/investing  
2) <strong>Warning cap</strong>: payment that forces you to cut discretionary spending and reduces flexibility  
3) <strong>Hard fail cap</strong>: payment that breaks your plan (no buffer, forced liquidation, or chronic arrears risk)

A simple starting point (adjust to your life) is:
- <strong>Target:</strong> mortgage payment ≤ 25% of monthly take-home  
- <strong>Warning:</strong> 25–35%  
- <strong>Hard fail:</strong> > 35% (or much lower if you have dependents, unstable income, or other debts)

<strong>One-line takeaway:</strong> If you don’t set caps now, you’ll set them later—under stress, with worse options.

## Misconception box: “If I pass DSR today, I’m safe”
<strong>Misconception:</strong> “The bank approved me, so the mortgage is manageable.”  
<strong>Why it’s wrong:</strong> DSR is a snapshot under today’s assumptions. Variable-rate resets, spread widening, job/income variability, and other debt payments can change your effective burden fast. Also, DSR compliance doesn’t measure what matters most: whether you can keep your life plan (childcare, commuting, savings, emergency buffer) intact.  
<strong>Check this instead (2 quick checks):</strong>  
- <strong>DSR headroom:</strong> how much “room” remains if your rate rises +1%p or +2%p?  
- <strong>Liquidity runway:</strong> how many months can you cover the gap without selling assets at a bad time?

## The 10-minute checklist: turn anxiety into rules
Use this once to set your rules. Then reuse it whenever rates, income, or the dashboard regime changes.

### Checklist A: cash-flow & buffer (3 minutes)
- □ I know my <strong>monthly take-home</strong> (average of last 6 months, not best month)  
- □ I know my <strong>non-negotiable costs</strong> (housing non-mortgage + utilities + childcare + insurance + transport)  
- □ I have an <strong>emergency buffer</strong> that won’t disappear in one shock (cash + safe equivalents)  
- □ My plan still works if I reduce investing temporarily (I know what gets paused first)  

Tip for tightening your safety net: use <strong>this risk-based emergency fund guide</strong> to set a buffer that matches your debt and job risk:  
[Build a risk-based emergency fund that survives real shocks](/en/posts/personalFinance/emergency-fund-by-risk)

### Checklist B: loan structure & reset risk (4 minutes)
- □ I know whether my rate is <strong>fixed, variable, or hybrid</strong>, and the reset schedule  
- □ I know the <strong>remaining maturity</strong> and whether I’m exposed to a “refinance cliff”  
- □ I listed <strong>all debt payments</strong> (credit loans, car loans, student loans) because DSR counts them  
- □ I know my <strong>personal DSR cap</strong> (not just the bank’s cap) and my headroom  

### Checklist C: dashboard-driven price discipline (3 minutes)
Open <strong>/en/market/real-estate</strong> and answer:
- □ My candidate homes sit inside a clear <strong>price band</strong> (not “whatever looks okay”)  
- □ The last 6–12 months show a <strong>trend regime</strong> (downtrend, flat base, recovery)  
- □ Volume is <strong>confirming</strong> the move (recovery with volume vs bounce on thin trades)

<strong>One-line takeaway:</strong> Your mortgage plan is the intersection of (cash-flow caps) × (reset risk) × (dashboard discipline).

## Table 1: Dashboard metric → interpretation → next action
This table is your bridge from “market data” to “personal decision rules.”

| Dashboard metric (what you can read) | Interpretation (what it really means) | Next action (what you do) |
|---|---|---|
| Price distribution (median + upper/lower bands) | If your target is in the tail (upper band), liquidity and negotiation power drop; errors cost more | Rebuild budget with a tighter cap or widen search radius; add a “walk-away” rule |
| 6–12 month trend (slope + stability) | A stable base behaves differently from a sharp rebound; rebounds without volume are fragile | If rebound is thin, require stronger personal buffer; if base is stable, focus on affordability math |
| Transaction volume vs recent average | Rising volume confirms willingness to transact; falling volume increases gap risk (slow selling, slow refinancing) | If volume is weak, avoid stretching; keep more liquidity and negotiate harder |
| Price ↑ while volume ↓ | Often a “thin-market bounce,” not broad recovery | Treat as caution: stress-test +2%p and require extra headroom |
| Volume ↑ while price flat | “Absorption” phase; market is clearing without pushing prices yet | If your affordability passes, prioritize unit quality and terms over timing |
| Region/segment dispersion (some districts rising, others flat) | Micro-regimes; your experience is local, not headline | Use the dashboard to pick the micro-market you can afford, not the headline narrative |

Interpretation (how to use this table):
1) This is not for predicting price direction; it’s for deciding how strict your rules should be.  
2) When the dashboard signals fragility (thin volume, tail pricing), your required headroom should increase.  
3) When confirmation improves (volume + stable trend), you can focus more on unit/terms—still inside your caps.

## Build the stress table from a dashboard price band (the core exercise)
Here is the practical sequence:

1) Use the dashboard to select a <strong>candidate purchase price band</strong> (example: 700M / 900M / 1,100M KRW).  
2) Decide your financing structure (example below uses <strong>60% loan / 40% down</strong> for illustration).  
3) Stress-test monthly payments at <strong>current rate</strong> and <strong>+1%p / +2%p</strong>.  
4) Define a clear fail condition (e.g., payment > 35% of take-home, or DSR headroom turns negative after including other debts).

Interpretation (how to use this table):
1) Treat the <strong>+1%p and +2%p columns</strong> as “realistic stress,” not worst-case doom—variable resets and spreads can do this.  
2) Your decision is not “can I pay today?” but “can I pay after a shock without breaking life essentials?”  
3) If only the 4% column works and the 5% column breaks, you are one regime change away from stress—reduce price band or increase down payment.

<strong>One-line takeaway:</strong> A home is affordable only if it remains affordable under a rate regime you’ve already seen before.

## Scenario 1 (first-home): the “stretch” purchase that kills your plan
Profile (example):
- Couple with stable income, but high fixed costs (childcare + commuting)
- Savings are strong, but liquidity buffer is smaller than it looks (locked in deposits / investments)
- Wants a Seoul unit at the upper end of the dashboard distribution

Rules-based decision:
1) If the Seoul candidate sits in the <strong>upper distribution tail</strong> on the dashboard, treat it as low-margin: increase required headroom.  
2) Use the stress table: if +1%p already hits your warning cap, the purchase is “fragile.”  
3) If fragile, you don’t need a prediction—you need a redesign: smaller unit, different district, or higher down payment.

Practical “plan-preserving” rule:
<strong>Never buy a home that requires you to stop investing permanently just to stay current on payments.</strong> Temporary pauses happen. Permanent surrender is a sign the loan is too large.

## Scenario 2 (upgrader): the hidden risk is the transition, not the final home
Upgraders often underestimate transition risk:
- Overlap months (two housing costs)
- Uncertain sale timing (especially if local volume is weak)
- Bridge financing and refinancing timing risk

Rules-based decision:
- If dashboard volume is weakening, assume <strong>slower execution</strong> (sell takes longer, terms worsen).  
- In that regime, you either (a) reduce target price band, or (b) increase liquidity buffer, or (c) delay upgrade until your execution risk is lower.

A strong upgrader rule:
<strong>Upgrade only when you can carry the overlap period without borrowing more.</strong>

## The dashboard-driven “support vs break” framework for households
In markets, people ask “support vs break.” Households should ask the same—but in personal terms:

- <strong>Support:</strong> your payment stays under your warning cap even after +1%p; you keep buffers; you can continue minimum investing  
- <strong>Break:</strong> +1%p pushes you beyond hard fail or kills buffers; you need hope/refinancing to survive

Observable triggers you can track weekly (no forecasting needed):
- <strong>Mortgage rates / bank loan rates</strong>: are they drifting or stepping up?  
- <strong>Financial conditions / spreads</strong>: when spreads widen, refinancing becomes harder and resets can sting  
- <strong>Transaction volume</strong> on the dashboard: thin volume increases execution risk (selling, moving, renegotiating)

A simple rule:
<strong>If two of three deteriorate (rates up + spreads up, or rates up + volume down), tighten your purchase band or increase down payment.</strong>

## FAQs: mortgage risk, DSR, variable rates, and dashboard use

### 1) What does DSR mean in simple terms for a household plan?
DSR is a debt-service ratio concept: how much of your income is committed to debt payments. For planning, the key is not the exact regulatory formula but your <strong>personal DSR headroom</strong>—how much room you have if rates rise or income dips. Build rules that keep you away from the cliff.

### 2) How should I choose my “target payment cap”?
Start from take-home pay, not gross income. Many households use a target cap around 25% and a hard fail cap around 35%, but your number should be lower if you have dependents, unstable income, or high fixed costs. The best cap is the one that keeps your plan (buffers + investing) alive.

### 3) Why is +1%p / +2%p stress testing so important right now?
Because variable-rate resets and refinancing conditions can shift quickly when spreads widen. Even if the policy headline sounds calm, borrower rates can move differently. Stress testing is not pessimism—it’s basic resilience math.

### 4) Should I avoid variable-rate mortgages entirely?
Not necessarily. The question is whether you can survive the reset schedule and whether you have the buffer to absorb a period of higher payments. If variable makes your +1%p column fail, you’re borrowing too close to the edge.

### 5) How do I use the dashboard if I’m not a market expert?
Treat the dashboard as an input selector: pick a candidate price band from distribution, check the 6–12 month trend regime, and confirm volume. You are not trying to predict the next move. You are using observable conditions to decide how strict your borrowing rules should be.

### 6) What does “volume confirms recovery” mean for a home buyer?
If transactions rise while prices stabilize or gradually improve, the market is absorbing supply and liquidity is healthier. Thin volume with rising prices can be fragile. For buyers, healthier volume reduces execution risk (selling, negotiating, refinancing).

### 7) I’m an upgrader—what’s the biggest mistake I can make?
Underestimating transition risk: overlap months, uncertain sale timing, and bridge financing costs. Use the dashboard volume signal to decide how conservative you should be. If volume is weak, assume slower execution and keep more liquidity.

### 8) What’s the simplest rule to avoid overbuying?
<strong>If your mortgage requires “perfect months” to survive, it’s too big.</strong> Your plan should survive a few normal shocks: rate +1%p, small income dip, or higher living costs—without forcing you to abandon buffers and long-term investing.

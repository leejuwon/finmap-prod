## Compounding and recurring investing can happen in the same portfolio

Compounding means earlier returns become part of the base for later growth. DCA means investing fixed amounts at regular intervals. A lump sum can compound, and each recurring deposit can compound for the time it remains invested. These are compatible concepts, not opposing investment products.

The [SEC's Investor.gov explanation of dollar-cost averaging](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/glossary/dollar-cost-averaging) describes regular equal-dollar investing through changing prices. That changes the quantity purchased at each price. It does not establish that recurring investing always beats investing money already available.

Separate those funding situations. Investing $500 from each future paycheck is not equivalent to owning ten years of those deposits today. Entering the future $60,000 as today's initial capital gives money market exposure before you have earned it. A comparison can be mathematically correct and still answer the wrong financial question.

If the underlying growth concept is unfamiliar, start with [how simple and compound growth differ](/en/posts/personalFinance/simple-vs-compound). Here the focus is selecting a tool and making its assumptions useful.

### Define the three input groups before opening a calculator

<div class="callout">
<p><strong>Available initial capital:</strong> money you can invest now.<br />Measure it after separating near-term spending and emergency cash.<br />Do not add future paycheck contributions to the current balance.</p>
<p><strong>Recurring contribution:</strong> new money per chosen deposit interval.<br />Check the amount against actual cash flow and its annual total.<br />The same numeric entry means something different when changed from monthly to weekly.</p>

## Choose the first tool by the result you need to inspect

| Planning question | Compound calculator | DCA calculator | Useful starting point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Initial capital plus new deposits | Initial balance and monthly contributions | Initial balance and recurring deposits | Either; recurring deposits are not exclusive to DCA |
| Purchasing power | Inflation and present-value analysis | Main focus is nominal contribution growth | Compound first when future spending power matters |
| Deposit scheduling | Monthly base model and contribution scenario views | Monthly or weekly deposits and annual increases | DCA first for a changing deposit plan |
| Horizon and growth conventions | Monthly versus annual comparison view | Monthly or yearly return conversion mode | Read the conventions before comparing outputs |
| Goals and downside cases | Goal-related analysis, sensitivity, yearly table | Required contributions, yearly table, drawdown scenarios | DCA first for deposit sustainability and shock timing |
| Currency, tax, fees | User inputs and KRW/USD | User inputs and KRW/USD | Neither determines your account's actual tax treatment |

Open the [compound interest calculator](/en/tools/compound-interest) for a baseline future value and purchasing-power check. Open the [DCA calculator](/en/tools/dca-calculator) for recurring contributions, contribution increases, and downside comparisons. Both accept an initial portfolio as well as new contributions.

The annual comparison panel in the compound tool and the yearly mode in DCA should not be assumed identical. A feature name does not define the entire convention. Record whether the displayed annual rate is divided into periods or converted as an effective annual rate, then inspect when deposits receive their first return.

## Compare $300, $500, and $1,000 a month on one stated basis

The following baseline uses no initial investment, end-of-month deposits, a hypothetical 5% nominal annual return divided by 12, and zero tax, fees, inflation, or contribution increases. This matches the compound tool's basic monthly timing. Values are rounded to the nearest dollar.

| Horizon (years) | $300/month final value (USD) | $500/month final value (USD) | $1,000/month final value (USD) | Decision to inspect |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 5 | 20,402 | 34,003 | 68,006 | How much of the goal must come from deposits? |
| 10 | 46,585 | 77,641 | 155,282 | Can the contribution survive changing household costs? |
| 20 | 123,310 | 205,517 | 411,034 | How sensitive is the plan to long-term assumptions? |

At $500 a month, deposits total $30,000, $60,000, and $120,000 respectively. The final balance includes those deposits; it is not all investment profit. A longer horizon increases the modeled compounding effect but also extends the period over which income, taxes, inflation, and market returns can change.

The monthly end-of-period formula is <strong>FV = P × (1+i)^n + C × ((1+i)^n − 1) ÷ i</strong>, where P is initial capital, C is the monthly deposit, i is the monthly rate, and n is the deposit count. At a zero rate, use P+C×n. Beginning-of-period deposits add one additional period of growth to the contribution portion.

Doubling contributions doubles this model's result when all other inputs remain unchanged. That does not mean doubling is affordable. A smaller amount maintained through ordinary expenses can be more useful than a large standing instruction repeatedly reversed to cover bills.

## Reconcile timing before interpreting a difference as better performance

The current compound base model applies monthly growth and then adds the contribution. The current DCA model adds the period's contribution before applying growth. With no costs or taxes, a $500 monthly contribution at the same 5% nominal rate over ten years produces about $77,641 under end-of-month timing and $77,965 under beginning-of-period timing.

That roughly $324 difference reflects time in the model. It is not evidence that one investing strategy generates extra return for the same cash flow. Match timing before trying to explain the gap with market arguments.

| Assumption | Compound base model | DCA base model | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Contribution timing | Growth, then monthly deposit | Deposit, then period growth | End versus beginning of period |
| Annual fee | Deducted from the annual return input | Deducted after the return is adjusted by tax | Rate units and calculation order |
| Tax | Applied to positive cumulative investment gain at the end | Applied as a reduction in the return used each period | Deferral versus ongoing return reduction |
| Rate conversion | Basic monthly rate is annual nominal rate divided by 12 | Monthly mode divides by period count; yearly mode converts an effective annual rate | Nominal and effective rates are different inputs |

Tax assumptions can outweigh the timing advantage. For an initial $10,000 plus $500 a month over ten years, a 5% return, 15.4% illustrative tax input, and a 0.5% annual fee produce approximately $87,994 in the compound model and $87,324 in the DCA monthly model. The tax percentage is a comparison input, not a statement about US capital-gains tax, retirement accounts, or any particular investment.

Actual taxation can involve account-specific rules, distributions, realized gains, losses, exemptions, and filing circumstances. These tools do not determine those rules for you. A lower modeled tax cost is not an account recommendation. For a clean diagnostic, first compare zero-tax and zero-fee results, then add your assumptions and record the remaining model differences.

## Two investors need different first checks

### Case 1: Existing savings and a future spending goal

Taylor has $30,000 available to invest and can add $300 a month. The main question is whether the projected balance will cover a purchase in ten years in today's purchasing power. Taylor starts with the compound tool, records a zero-inflation baseline, and then checks a separate inflation assumption while holding deposits and the return assumption constant.

If the goal falls short, Taylor changes one input at a time. Increasing the deposit, extending the deadline, and raising the assumed return simultaneously would hide what closed the gap. A return assumption is uncertain; a deadline and an affordable deposit are household decisions.

### Case 2: A household planning gradual contribution increases

Morgan and Alex start at $500 a month and want to test 3% annual contribution increases over twenty years. They use DCA, where that increase belongs in the contribution-growth field rather than the investment-return field. The second year's monthly deposit would be $515. They also save a 0% increase baseline because future pay raises are not guaranteed.

They inspect the goal shortfall and drawdown cases, not just the highest ending balance. The base model uses a smooth hypothetical price index starting at 100 rather than actual ETF prices. The shock cases are illustrations of timing effects, not forecasts of how quickly markets recover. A zero modeled drawdown in a smooth rising base case does not mean the investment is risk-free.

For weekly deposits, they recalculate the amount per deposit. Maintaining $6,000 per year requires about $115.38 across 52 deposits, subject to rounding. Entering $500 as a weekly contribution would change annual contributions to $26,000. The frequency setting alone does not preserve the monthly budget.

## Set conditions for maintaining, increasing, or revising the plan

| Planning state | Observable trigger | Analysis to start with | Action and review condition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Maintain | Income and recurring spending remain stable | Compound baseline and purchasing power | Keep the contribution and schedule a periodic review |
| Increase | Repeated surplus remains after emergency and near-term needs | DCA with 0% versus proposed annual contribution growth | Raise deposits within confirmed cash flow, then review next year |
| Rebuild | Leave, a missed contribution, or an earlier spending deadline changes the budget | Lower DCA deposits and a shorter compound horizon | Revise deposits, goal, or deadline before relying on the old result |

These are household operating states rather than market predictions. A rising balance does not automatically justify raising deposits. A near-term cash requirement can matter more than a favorable return assumption. Use sensitivity analysis to identify the assumption that breaks the plan, then decide what you can actually change.

## Turn an estimate into a plan over the next week

In 15 minutes, write down available initial capital, an affordable recurring contribution, and the spending date. Choose the first tool by the main question. Check the zero-return contribution total to catch unit and frequency errors before interpreting investment gains.

Use 60 minutes to save a baseline, change the horizon, then change the return assumption separately. Reproduce the common inputs in the other tool with costs and taxes off. Record timing differences before reintroducing cost assumptions. Keep the scenario conditions with the result so a later comparison remains meaningful.

Within one week, compare the proposed deposit with actual pay and upcoming expenses. Decide when to increase, reduce, or pause it. Entering a number in a calculator does not schedule a bank transfer or place an investment order.

- □ Initial capital excludes emergency and near-term spending money.
- □ Deposit frequency and annual contribution totals match the budget.
- □ Nominal/effective rate and deposit timing are recorded.
- □ Tax and fee assumptions are separate from actual tax eligibility.
- □ A lower-return and shorter-horizon case has been inspected.
- □ A review date and contribution-change rule are written down.

## Questions about choosing a compound or DCA calculator

### Do monthly investors have to use a DCA calculator?

No. FinMap's compound calculator also supports monthly contributions. Start with the view that addresses purchasing power, scheduling, or downside timing most directly.

### Is the compound calculator only for lump sums?

No. It accepts both an initial balance and monthly contributions. You can also use a zero initial balance to model new monthly deposits.

### Why do the same inputs produce different totals?

Deposit timing, tax timing, and rate conversion can differ. First compare monthly settings with no tax, fees, or increases. Then add assumptions one at a time.

### Does DCA use historical ETF prices?

The base simulation uses a constant assumed return and an illustrative price index. Shock cases add a specified price disturbance. They do not reproduce a chosen ETF's actual history.

### Does switching from monthly to weekly preserve my budget?

Not automatically. Recalculate the amount per deposit and check the annual total. The same numeric amount paid weekly represents much more contributed money.

### Can the tax setting calculate my US after-tax result?

It applies a simplified model to the input percentage. It does not determine account eligibility, filing status, distributions, or applicable tax law. Use the result as an assumption-based comparison.

### Can I raise the return assumption to make my goal work?

You can explore sensitivity, but a higher assumption is not a reliable funding source. Compare lower returns and consider the contribution, target amount, or deadline you can actually adjust.

### Which result should I check last?

For a spending goal, inspect purchasing power and the deadline. For a recurring investment plan, inspect contribution reductions and downside cases. Keep the cash-flow requirements beside the final balance.
